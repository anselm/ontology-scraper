# How a thing gets a kind

When the machine reads an article it names each thing it finds and says what basic, everyday kind it most resembles: a container ship, a locality, a local government, a petition. That label is a prototype. If no prototype by that name exists yet, one is created on the spot, under the right top kind.

So on day one the list of prototypes is long and ragged: towns and cities and localities side by side, a "development application" next to a "petition". That is the raw material, not the result.

Periodically an induction pass looks at the whole list. For each top kind it asks a model to merge true synonyms, to say which broader kind each prototype belongs under, and to flag labels that are really roles someone holds for a while (minister, captain) rather than kinds of thing. Roles get demoted to a component on the person. A prototype is only worth keeping if it predicts something its parent does not: a container ship carries cargo capacity and a flag in a way a plain ship does not.

Each prototype also learns its normative bundle: of the things called by that name, what share have a location, a lifespan, an office, a stated capacity. That bundle is what "a typical ship" means here, in the sense cognitive science gives the word: a cluster of correlated features, not a definition.

A thing's kind is a scored claim, not a fact. It carries who said it, how many sources agree, and a rank. When the kind was once true and no longer is, it is dated rather than deleted.
